You walked into your first week of cohort, opened a Python tutorial, did the lessons, and at the end of the evening you'd typed about three hundred lines of code without writing more than thirty yourself. You completed every exercise. You read every explanation. And the next morning you opened a blank file and couldn't remember how to start.

That feeling — competent while reading, lost when alone — is not a sign you're slow. It's a sign that almost everything written about "how to learn to code" was designed for someone who isn't you.

## The material is shaped wrong for you

Most learn-to-code resources assume their reader is twenty, in full-time education, with twelve unbroken hours a week to dedicate to the craft. That's not most adult learners in a DSAI cohort. You have a job, or a household, or both. You get an hour and a half between dinner and bed, four times a week, if you're lucky. You are sharper than an undergraduate at reading the abstract; you are slower at memorising syntax; you have more context for *why* this matters and less patience for material that doesn't respect your time.

When the material is shaped wrong, the cost lands on you. You feel slow. You blame yourself. The truth is the material was shaped for someone else.

> 💡 **Permission, up front.** If a tutorial assumes you can sit for four hours straight or "just keep going until you finish chapter twelve", the problem is the tutorial, not you. Find a different one. There are many.

## What the research actually says

Cognitive science has known how adults learn skills for about forty years now. Three findings consistently show up across studies, and they map directly onto how to use your one-and-a-half evening hours well.

**Spaced practice beats marathon practice.** Three half-hour sessions across three evenings consolidate more than one ninety-minute session. The hours are the same; the brain treats them differently. Your sleep does the lifting between sessions.

**Retrieval beats re-reading.** Trying to recall something you half-remember, even badly, builds the memory more than reading it for the second time. This is why people who close the book and try to summarise what they read learn faster than people who highlight everything.

**Desirable difficulty beats smooth learning.** A lesson that's a little too hard, that you have to struggle through, leaves a deeper mark than a lesson that flows easily. This is why tutorials that hold your hand all the way through feel great in the moment and leave nothing behind.

These three together explain why "I followed the tutorial perfectly" and "I learned what was in the tutorial" are not the same statement.

## Three habits that separate the students who get fluent

Across the cohorts I've taught, the students who end up fluent share three quiet habits the others don't. None of them are about working harder.

**Minimum viable practice rotation.** Touch four small things this week, not one big thing. A Python list comprehension on Monday, a SQL join on Tuesday, a FastAPI route on Wednesday, a Postgres query on Thursday. Rotate; don't bury yourself in one topic. Spaced practice, applied to your week.

**Code-aloud reading.** When you read a piece of code you didn't write, read it out loud — actually out loud, in a private room if you need to. The voice exposes the spots where you're skimming over a line you don't understand. The eye lies to you; the voice doesn't.

**An "I'm confused because" journal.** When something doesn't make sense, write down — in your own words, not the tutorial's — exactly what's confusing you. One paragraph. The act of naming the confusion is half the answer. The other half usually arrives within two days.

> The students who quietly drift away from a cohort almost never drift because the material was too hard. They drift because they never built a private practice that fits their life, and "going to class" was the only learning they did. Build the private practice in week one.

## What this lesson is asking of you

Nothing dramatic. Just two small things, starting this week.

First: stop blaming yourself for being slow. The material is shaped wrong for you. A few of the habits above will move you to the same fluency the full-timers eventually reach — sometimes faster, because you're bringing more context to the problem.

Second: pick one of the three habits and try it for ten days. Not all three. One. The rotation, the reading-aloud, or the journal — whichever sounds least painful. After ten days you'll know whether it's earning its place.
