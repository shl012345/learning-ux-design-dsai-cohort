The last lesson, written for the week the cohort ends — the week most learners quietly drift away from the craft because no one is telling them what's next.

Twelve months from now you can either be in a noticeably better position than the day after graduation, or in roughly the same place. The difference is rarely talent. It's almost always whether you kept the habits going through the months when no one was checking up on you.

This lesson is the realistic roadmap: three small things to build, three reading habits that compound, and the things to politely ignore.

## What to build — three small projects, not a fourth course

The instinct after a cohort is to enrol in another cohort. Resist it. The marginal return on a second course is small; the marginal return on building things is large. Three small projects across the next year:

**One solo project.** Something modest you build alone, deploy, and use yourself. The smaller and more useful, the better. A script that reads your email and summarises it. A dashboard for a hobby. A small RAG over your own notes. The point is to build the whole thing — design, code, deploy, debug, maintain — without anyone helping you. That experience is what closes the gap between "took a cohort" and "actually builds things".

**One project with a friend.** Same scale, two people. The work doubles in complexity because now you have to communicate, agree on a shape, share code, resolve disagreements. This is the practice that makes you employable on a team. Pick someone whose schedule roughly matches yours and whose interests overlap with yours, even loosely.

**One project that scratches a real itch.** Something annoying in your own life that you fix with code. A scheduling problem. A repeated chore. A spreadsheet you'd love to never touch again. The constraint that it's a *real* itch — not a manufactured project for the portfolio — does two things: it forces realism, and it gives you a story to tell in interviews that doesn't sound like every other career-switcher's story.

Three projects across twelve months is one project every four months. That's slow enough to be sustainable and fast enough to compound.

## What to read — three habits that compound

The DSAI information firehose is endless. You cannot consume it; you can only choose what shape of attention to give it. Three reading habits that consistently produce something:

**One paper or substantive blog per week.** Not the headlines. Not the LinkedIn posts. One actual piece of writing — a paper, a long-form blog, a chapter of a book — where you slow down enough to take notes. Forty minutes. Saturday morning if your weekdays are busy.

**One real repo read per fortnight.** Open someone else's working codebase and apply the four-pass technique from lesson 3. Spend an hour. Write down what you understood, what confused you, what design choices you'd have made differently. This habit alone is what most cohort students are missing.

**One written reflection per month.** Open a notebook somewhere — a blog post, a draft email to yourself, a section of your fork from lesson 15. Write 500 words on the most surprising thing you learned this month. The act of writing forces the consolidation that reading alone doesn't.

Notice what's not on the list: every newsletter, every X thread, every new model release. The noise level on those is high and the durability is zero. A piece of news you read on Tuesday is gone by Friday. A repo you read on Saturday is in your head for years.

> 💡 **A small reframe.** Your job is no longer to keep up with everything. Your job is to develop *taste* — the ability to look at a new tool, paper, or framework and quickly tell whether it's worth your attention. Taste comes from a small number of deep reads, not a large number of shallow ones. Bias hard toward depth.

## What not to chase

There are three temptations that consistently eat the year of a fresh DSAI career-switcher without producing much.

**Every new model release.** A new model came out this week. There will be another one next week. Reading about them is fun and produces almost nothing. The skill that compounds is "be good at using whichever model is current", not "always be using the newest one". Pick a model that works, learn it deeply, swap when there's a concrete reason.

**Every new framework.** LangChain. Autogen. CrewAI. The next one. Each has fans, each will be displaced. Frameworks are leverage *after* you understand the underlying ideas; they're a tax *before* you understand them. Build one project in raw Python before you adopt a framework. You'll know what the framework is doing — and whether you need it.

**Every job posting that wants seven years of experience.** Job ads are wish lists, not requirements. Apply for jobs whose underlying skills you can demonstrate, even if you don't match the years-of-experience number. The honest career-switcher cover letter says: "I have eighteen months of focused practice; here are three things I've built; I learn fast; here's why I want this specific role". That works more often than people expect.

## The reframe at the end

Twelve months sounds long when you're at week one of the after-period and short when you're at week fifty. The habits above are how you make week fifty look like progress rather than drift.

You don't need permission from anyone to keep going. You don't need another certificate. You don't need to wait until you "feel ready". You need to ship the next small thing, read the next deep piece, write the next reflection, and let the months do the work.

> The cohort gave you the starting kit. Whether you build a body of work on top of it is now entirely yours to decide.
