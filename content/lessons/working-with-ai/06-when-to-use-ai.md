Cohort students often use AI for everything. They prompt their way through the tutorial, prompt their way through the exercise, prompt their way through the debugging, prompt their way through the README. Three months later they discover they can't reproduce any of it without the model present, and the cohort certificate sits in a drawer doing nothing for their actual employability.

The question isn't *can* you use AI for this. You almost always can. The question is *should* you, given what you're trying to walk out of this cohort with.

## Three modes where AI consistently helps

Some tasks AI assistance compounds your learning. The pattern: the AI does the part that isn't the point, freeing you to do the part that is.

| Mode | What you're doing | Why AI helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Drafting** | First version of a thing — README, docstring, ticket description, design doc | Producing the blank-page draft is the hard part. Editing is the easy part. Let AI produce, you edit |
| **Exploring** | "How might I approach this?" "What are the options for X?" | AI gives you the shape of the space faster than searching can. You make the choice |
| **Rubber-ducking** | You're stuck and want to explain the problem aloud | The act of explaining unblocks you, regardless of whether AI says anything useful |
| **Translating** | "Show me what this Python idiom looks like in TypeScript" | Mechanical translation between languages or libraries. Boring; AI does it correctly |

Notice the shape: in each mode, the AI does the part that isn't the point, leaving you to do the part that is. Drafting and editing are different skills; AI handles drafting, you handle editing. The skill you're building — the editorial judgement — strengthens.

## Three modes where AI consistently gets in your way

In other tasks AI assistance short-circuits the very thing you're trying to learn. The pattern: the AI does *the* thing that was the point, and the muscle you were building atrophies.

| Mode | What you're doing | Why AI gets in your way |
|---|---|---|
| **Assessed work** | Your cohort assignment, a take-home interview, anything where the assessor wants to know what *you* can do | Obvious one. Easy to rationalise away. Don't |
| **The point of this week's lesson** | A debugging exercise, a from-scratch implementation, the thing the instructor designed *because* the struggle is the teaching | If you skip the struggle, you skip the learning. The exercise has no other purpose |
| **First-time consolidation** | The README for a project you just finished, the explanation of a concept you're trying to lock in | Writing forces you to organise what you half-know into a coherent thing. AI-writing skips that step entirely |

## The one question that tells you which mode you're in

When you're unsure which side of the line you're on, ask yourself: **would I write this if AI didn't exist?**

If the honest answer is "yes, I'd do this anyway, I just want to do it faster" — that's the helping side. Use AI.

If the honest answer is "no, this is a chore I'd skip" — that's the warning side. Look harder at whether you actually need to do it, or whether you're using AI to avoid the learning the task was designed to give you.

The question is uncomfortable. That's why it works. The instinct to use AI is often the instinct to avoid friction; the question forces you to notice when the friction was the point.

## The two failure modes that hit cohort students hardest

I've watched these two patterns end careers-in-the-making, repeatedly.

**AI-completing a debugging exercise.** You hit an error. Instead of reading it, you paste it into the model. The model fixes the code. You move on. You did not build the mental model the debugging exercise was designed to give you — the model of *how errors propagate in your specific kind of system*. You will hit the next bug as helplessly as the first. Read the error first. Try the fix yourself first. Use AI to *explain*, not to *resolve*.

**AI-writing the README for a project you don't yet understand.** Your cohort project is finished and works. You're tired. You ask the AI to write the README. It produces something competent that nobody — including you — has to think about. The consolidation step that the README *was for* doesn't happen. You leave the cohort with a folder of working projects you couldn't talk about at an interview. Write the README yourself, badly, in twenty minutes. Then ask AI to tighten the prose. That's the right division.

## A small daily check

End of each evening, look back at the AI conversations you had today. For each, ask: helping or in the way? Be honest. The first time you do this, you'll be uncomfortable with at least one. That's useful information. Adjust tomorrow.
